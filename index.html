Core Learning Modules

General Provisions (Art. 1156)

An obligation is a juridical necessity to give, to do or not to do.

Active Subject (Creditor/Obligee)

The person who has the legal right to demand performance of the obligation.

Passive Subject (Debtor/Obligor)

The person bound to perform the prestation or duty under penalty of law.

Prestation (Object)

The particular conduct required (to give, to do, or not to do).

Juridical Tie (Vinculum Juris)

The efficient cause that binds the parties together legally.

5 Sources of Obligations (Art. 1157)

Directly imposed by statute. Cannot be presumed; must be explicitly stated in the Civil Code or special laws.

Example: Duty of spouses to support each other; payment of income tax.

Arise from the meeting of minds between two parties. Obligations arising from contracts have the force of law between the contracting parties (Art. 1159).

Example: Lease agreement to pay rent monthly.

Lawful, voluntary, and unilateral acts based on the principle that no one shall be unjustly enriched at the expense of another.

  • Negotiorum Gestio: Voluntary management of another's abandoned business or property.
  • Solutio Indebiti: Payment by mistake when there is no right to demand it.

Civil liability arising from criminal offenses under Art. 100 of the Revised Penal Code.

Includes: Restitution, Reparation of damage, and Indemnification for consequential damages.

Fault or negligence causing damage to another where there is no pre-existing contractual relation (Art. 2176).

Example: A reckless driver hitting a pedestrian crossing legally.

Primary Classification of Obligations

Pure vs. Conditional

Pure: Demandable at once, not subject to any condition.

Conditional: Acquisition or extinguishment depends upon a future and uncertain event.

With a Period / Term

Performance depends upon the arrival of a day certain which must necessarily come.

Alternative vs. Facultative

Alternative: Debtor has several prestations, completely performing one discharges obligation.

Facultative: Only one prestation agreed upon, but debtor may substitute another.

Joint vs. Solidary

Joint: Each debtor pays only their share; each creditor demands only theirs.

Solidary: Each debtor liable for entire obligation; each creditor can claim all.

Modes of Extinguishing Obligations (Art. 1231)

1. Payment or Performance: Complete delivery of object or fulfillment of service.
2. Loss of the Thing Due: Specific object perishes without fault of debtor.
3. Condonation / Remission: Gratuitous abandonment of right by creditor.
4. Confusion / Merger: Characters of creditor and debtor merged in one person.
5. Compensation: Two persons are mutual debtors and creditors of each other.
6. Novation: Substitution or modification of an obligation by a subsequent valid one.

4 Classes of Defective Contracts

1. Rescissible Contracts

Valid until Rescinded

Valid contract but causes economic lesion or financial damage to a party or third person.

Example: Contracts entered into in fraud of creditors.

2. Voidable Contracts

Valid until Annulled

Binding until annulled by court. Defect lies in vitiated consent (Mistake, Violence, Intimidation, Undue Influence, Fraud).

3. Unenforceable Contracts

Cannot be Enforced in Court

Cannot be sued upon unless ratified. Includes agreements failing Statute of Frauds or unauthorized acts.

4. Void or Inexistent

No Legal Effect At All

Produces no legal effect from beginning ($nulum$). Cannot be ratified or validated by passage of time.
